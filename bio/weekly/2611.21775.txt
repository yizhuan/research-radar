Rule-based curation and validation in batch fermentation of a genome-scale metabolic
model of the mycoprotein fungusFusarium venenatumA3/5
Jaewook Lee, Thomas Vinestock, Miao Guo∗
Department of Engineering, Faculty of Natural, Mathematical & Engineering Sciences, King’s College London, Strand, London, WC2R 2LS, United Kingdom
Abstract
Sustainable protein supply is a central food-system challenge, and mycoprotein fromFusarium venenatumis an established,
low-impact option. Its production relies on refined glucose, and yields on lower-cost feedstocks are normally found by fermentation.
Genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) can estimate these yields before any experiment, but to our knowledge none has been
published for this fungus, and models transferred from related species inherit template errors. Here we built iJL1143, a GEM of
strain A3/5, from anAspergillus nigermodel and curated it with rules based on homology, thermodynamics and the literature. We
compared it with the uncurated Draft model in four oxygen-enriched batch fermentations on glucose and fructose. Thermodynamic
directionality rules and the direction of the mitochondrial GTP/GDP carrier brought the maximum ATP yield of the Draft model
within the theoretical range. As a result, with sequential sugar uptake and no fitted parameters, the mean final-biomass discrepancy
fell from 20% for the Draft model to 6% for iJL1143. Sequential uptake and a fitted lag time also reduced its biomass time-course
error from 4.88 to 0.42 gL−1. In oxygen-enriched batch fermentation, iJL1143 can therefore estimate obtainable biomass before
fermentation, which could reduce the experiments needed to develop lower-cost mycoprotein.

1. Introduction
Mycoprotein produced by aerobic fermentation ofFusarium
venenatumA3/5 is an established food protein with lower green-
house gas emissions and land use than many livestock-derived
proteins [1–3]. Industrial production relies primarily on refined
glucose [4]. The biomass formed per unit of sugar influences
boththechoiceoffeedstockandthecostoftheprocess. Glucose
and fructose mixtures from sucrose hydrolysates and from food
and beverage side-streams offer candidate low-cost feedstocks
[4]. A model that estimates biomass yield and the course of a
fermentation on such feedstocks could reduce the experiments
needed to assess them.
Kinetic models describe such fermentations closely, but they
take their yields from the data they fit. A dual-substrate Monod
model of glucose and fructose fermentations used 15 fitted
parameters, two of them yield coefficients [4]. A yield fitted in
this way cannot be estimated before the experiment.
Genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) instead estimate
yield from the stoichiometry and energetics of the metabolic
network [5,6]. Dynamic flux balance analysis (dFBA) solves a
linear programme at each time step and uses the resulting fluxes
toupdatebiomassandextracellularconcentrationsthroughmass
balances [7].

with the uncurated draft then shows what the rules contribute.
Oxygen-enriched batch fermentation offers a clean first test,
because a growth-maximising model is most defensible when
oxygen does not limit growth.
We therefore hypothesised that, in oxygen-enriched batch
fermentation, rule-based curation alone would bring predicted

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Model reconstruction and curation
2.1.1. Template model and orthology mapping
Template-based reconstruction builds a draft model for a
new species by transferring the reactions of an existing model
whose genes have homologues in the new species [11]. The
transfer therefore depends on how genes are paired between
the two genomes. Reciprocal best hits, pairs of genes in two
genomes that are each other’s best hit, appear to be the most
common working definition of orthology [12]. Orthogroups
extend orthology to several species as sets of genes descended
from a single gene in their last common ancestor [13], and so
also capture the one-to-many relationships that reciprocal best
hits cannot. The paired genes enter the model through gene–
protein–reaction associations, called gene associations here,
which connect genes, proteins and reactions through logical
AND and OR relationships [5].
The CBS 513.88 version of iJB1325 (2,320 reactions, 1,373
genes) served as the template [8]. We chose it because it is a
community-curated model of an industrial filamentous fungus
thatpassed373of471testsofgrowth,genedeletionandnetwork
function [8]. Its genes were mapped to theF. venenatumA3/5
proteome [14] by reciprocal best hits from blastp in BLAST+
2.16.0 [15], with at least 40% identity, at least 70% query
coverage and𝐸≤10 −30. These cut-offs follow those used to
build thePenicillium chrysogenummodel with RAVEN, except
foracoverageof70%insteadof50%andnominimumalignment
length [16]. To check these pairs and to find orthologs that a
one-to-one search misses, orthogroups were also built across
10 fungal proteomes with OrthoFinder 2.5.5, with DIAMOND
2.2.8 as the sequence aligner [13, 17]. For the Curated model,
templategeneswithoutareciprocalbesthitwerethenmappedto
theF.venenatummembersoftheirorthogroup. Geneassociations
were rewritten as Boolean rules overF. venenatumgenes, and a
complex was retained when at least one of its subunits had an
ortholog. Nine subunits of complexes, in nine reactions, had no
ortholog. To search for a homologue that the reciprocal search
had missed, each was aligned against the whole proteome by
Smith–Waterman local alignment [18]. The alignment used the
BLOSUM62 matrix, gap penalties of 11 for opening and 1 for
extension, and the same identity and coverage thresholds. Only
the sulfur carrier of molybdopterin synthase matched a protein
of the same function, which took its place in the association, so
theotherassociationsnameonlythesubunitsthathaveorthologs.
Finally, to assess the evidence behind each association, the
EnzymeCommission(EC)numberofthereactionwascompared
with the UniProt EC annotation of its gene [19].
2.1.2. Rule-based curation
Curation converts a draft reconstruction into a curated one
by re-evaluating its content [5]. It adds reactions missing from
the network by gap-filling and defines the exchange reactions,
which represent the system boundary through which metabolites
enter and leave [5]. Curation also sets the direction of each
reaction, and thermodynamics provides a measure for this in
the reversibility index,Γ, the range of metabolite concentrations
required to reverse a reaction [20]. The further Γ is from 1,
the more irreversible the reaction. A value of 1,000 means
that reversal would need concentrations spanning three orders
of magnitude, about 3 µM to 3 mM around 100 µM [20].
eQuilibrator estimates the standard Gibbs energies behind the
index with the component contribution method [21, 22]. Finally,
curationsetstheenergycostofmaintenance,representedasATP
hydrolysis, growth-associated within the biomass reaction and
non-growth-associated in a reaction of its own [5]. To compare
predicted growth with observed yields, biomass is defined to
have a molecular weight of 1 gmmol−1 [23].

Both models retain the respiratory stoichiometry of the tem-

number of ATP formed per oxygen atom reduced. In the tem-
plate stoichiometry, each ATP delivered to the cytosol uses 4.88
protons, 3.88 at ATP synthase and one at the ADP/ATP carrier.
With 12 protons pumped per NADH and 8 per succinate, this
results in P/O ratios of 2.46 and 1.64. This stoichiometry was
retained because these ratios lie close to the measured mito-
chondrial ratios of about 2.5 and 1.5 [34] and to the 2.64 and
1.64 set in iMA871 [29], the source of the growth-associated
maintenance. Because these ratios are taken from the template
and were not measured inF. venenatum, lower P/O ratios were
tested in the sensitivity analysis (Section 2.2.2), but iJL1143
retains the template values.
2.1.3. Draft model
The Draft model is the comparator for every test (Fig. 1a). It
representstheuncuratedstartingpointofareconstruction,which
can contain reactions that curation later removes [5]. The Draft
model therefore retains every reaction of the template model,
with gene associations rewritten through reciprocal best hits
only. To isolate the effect of the network, it takes the biomass
composition and maintenance of the Curated model, with non-
growth-associated maintenance as one added reaction, so the
two models differ in their network and gene associations and
not in biomass or maintenance. A draft built with an orthology
filter would lack 85 template reactions, nine of which Stage 2
restores. With these nine retained, this filter changes neither the
maximum ATP yield nor the biomass yield of the Draft model
(Supplementary Table S3), so the choice of draft does not affect
the yield comparison.

2.2.2. Energy tests, stage removal, sensitivity and oxygen limita-
tion
To test whether the energetics of each model stay within
physical limits, its maximum ATP yield, the largest amount
of ATP it can form per mol glucose, was computed on aerobic
minimalmediumwithnomaintenance. Energy-generatingcycles
were tested by asking whether a cofactor can be recharged with
no substrate uptake [10], for nucleoside triphosphates, reduced
cofactors and the mitochondrial proton gradient. Two further
cycle tests asked whether NADH can be converted to NADPH
at no cost, in the cytosol and in mitochondria, which are the
routes that the redox rules of Stage 4 target. To find the effect
of each curation stage, the reactions listed under that stage in
the change log were returned to their Draft state in the Curated
model. Separately, the same reactions were given their Curated
state in the Draft model (Supplementary Table S3). Because the
two sets of directionality rules might substitute for each other,
they were also removed together from the Curated model. In the
Draft model, the carrier rule was combined with each set and
with both, and these models were run in the batch fermentations
like the Draft and Curated models.
Several inputs are borrowed or derived rather than measured
in this strain. The sensitivity of the yield to each was therefore
computed at the maximum sugar uptake rate of the unchanged
model,withO 2 notlimiting(SupplementaryNote2,Supplemen-
tary Fig. S2). Each macromolecular mass fraction was changed
by±25%, with the other fractions rescaled so that biomass stays
1 gmmol−1 [40], and each ATP cost was changed by the same
amount. The P/O ratio for NADH was reduced to 2.25 and
2.0 by increasing the number of protons used at ATP synthase.

2.2.3. Medium pH model
Medium pH was computed from a charge balance over phos-
phate,ammoniumandthemeasuredorganicacids,withoutfitted
parameters. A charge balance finds the pH at which the charges
of all ions in a solution sum to zero, so pH follows once the
amount of each ion is known. Growth changes these amounts
in two ways. One proton is released per ammonium nitrogen
assimilated,asmeasuredinyeastchemostats[ 43],andphosphate
and sulfate are taken up according to the biomass composition
(6.25 mmol N, 0.38 mmol P and 0.07 mmol S per gDW). Be-
cause the ionic strength of the medium is high, 0.446 M, activity
coefficients were taken from the Davies equation for electrolytes
in water [44], which gives an apparent phosphate p𝐾a2 of 6.79.
The balance was driven by the online biomass of each replicate,
and the net charge of the ions it does not track was set once from
the initial pH of each replicate. No other quantity was adjusted,
and the effects were added in an order fixed beforehand, so that
no step could be tuned to the measured pH.

part of it is the phosphorus of nucleic acids and lipids, which the
model includes.

2.3. Batch fermentation data
2.3.1. Fermentations and online measurements
Thebatchfermentationsonglucoseandfructosewerereported
in our previous work [4].F. venenatumA3/5 was inoculated at
10,000 spores per mL into 50 mL of Rank Hovis McDougall
(RHM) medium in 250 mL baffled shake flasks at 28°C and
220 rpm. Total sugar was 15 gL−1 in four conditions that differ
in the ratio of glucose to fructose, written Glc 15, Glc 10 +
Fru 5, Glc 5 + Fru 10 and Fru 15 (glucose and fructose in
gL−1), with three replicates each. To increase oxygen trans-
fer, the humidified headspace contained 200% of the oxygen
of air, and the volumetric mass transfer coefficient,𝑘L𝑎, was
96 h−1. With twice the air-saturation O2 concentration of water
at 28°C, this corresponds to a maximum oxygen transfer rate
of 46.9 mmolL−1h−1, which bounds O2 uptake in the models
(Section 2.2.1). Online biomass, pH and dissolved O2 were
recorded with PreSens shake flask readers. Online biomass is
inferred from reflectance and was calibrated to the start and end
points of each replicate. Online readings and HPLC samples are
placed on a common time axis, which starts at the first HPLC
sample.
2.3.2. Final biomass and yield
Yield is the gravimetric final biomass concentration divided
by the nominal initial sugar concentration. Final biomass was
measured by vacuum filtration and drying to constant mass at
65°C.Itsconcentrationisthedryweightdividedbytheeffective
final volume, which is the initial volume minus all sampled
volumes (pH, HPLC and biomass). Biomass and sugars do
not evaporate, so water loss does not affect this value. One
Fru 15 replicate lost 18.6 mL more volume than was removed
by sampling, compared with 3.6 and 6.6 mL for the other two.
This replicate was included, and excluding it would change the
Fru 15 yield from 0.53 to 0.54 gg−1.

3.2.2. Sequential uptake and lag time improve agreement with
time courses
Both mixed fermentations used glucose before fructose
(Fig. 3b,c), an order that a growth-maximising model does
not reproduce (Section 2.2.1). In Glc 5 + Fru 10, for example,
glucose fell to 1.5 gL−1 by 37 h while fructose stayed near
10 gL−1. The base setting does not impose this order, because
each sugar has its own maximum uptake rate, and the simulated
mixed fermentations therefore grew at 0.38 h−1 compared with
0.20 h−1 on a single sugar.
Adding sequential uptake and then lag time reduced the time-
course RMSE several-fold (Fig. 3g). Sequential uptake reduced
the biomass RMSE of the Curated model from 4.88 gL−1 in the
base setting to 3.87 gL−1. Fitting the lag time then reduced the
biomass RMSE to 1.16 gL−1 with one shared lag time and to
0.42 gL−1 with one lag time per condition, more than tenfold
below the base setting. Over the same steps, its sugar RMSE
fell from 6.22 to 1.73 gL−1. The base setting reached half final
biomass 16–35 h early (Supplementary Fig. S1b), and fitting the
lag time removed most of this timing error. The timing error
was−8 to+7 h with one shared lag time and below 0.2 h with
one lag time per condition. Lag time alone, however, was not
sufficient. With simultaneous uptake, the two simulated mixed
fermentations still reached half final biomass 10 and 2 h early at
the lowest initial biomass tested (Supplementary Note 1).
The gain from these additions lies in the time courses and not
in final biomass, whereas curation improved biomass and not
sugars. The mean discrepancy in final biomass of the Curated
model stayed at 6% whether the lag time was assumed or fitted
(Supplementary Note 1). At the final setting, the Curated model
still followed biomass more closely than the Draft model, with
RMSEs of 0.42 and 0.75 gL−1, whereas their sugar RMSEs
differedbyabout0.01gL −1 (Fig.3g). Sugarerrorwaslargeston
Glc 15 and Fru 15 (Supplementary Table S5), where measured
sugar fell before online biomass rose (Fig. 3a,d).
Afittedkineticmodelofthesamefermentationswascloseron
sugars and not on biomass [4]. That model used 15 fitted param-
eters and reported mean absolute errors (MAEs) of 0.41 gL−1
for biomass and 0.57 gL−1 for substrates. The Curated model
at the final setting, with four fitted lag times, resulted in MAEs
of 0.23 and 1.31 gL−1 (Section 2.2.4), although the two studies
average replicates differently. Because lag time is fitted to these
data, this comparison shows agreement and not prediction.
3.3. Products and medium chemistry
3.3.1. Transient glycerol and arabitol lie outside the model
Ethanol and acetate were essentially absent. Ethanol stayed
below 0.01 gL−1 and acetate was not detected in any sample
(Section 2.3.3). Neither model predicted either product, in
agreement with these measurements.
Neither model predicted the transient glycerol or arabitol.
Glycerol formed only while glucose was present and peaked
near glucose depletion (Fig. 4a–d). It rose to 0.42–0.61 gL−1
in the three fermentations containing glucose but stayed below
0.01 gL−1 on Fru 15. After the peak it was removed again, and
bythelastsampleithadreturnedto0.02gL −1 orless. InGlc5+
Fru 10, it disappeared once glucose was depleted, while fructose
was still being consumed. Net removal in the first interval after
glucose depletion was 0.2–0.9 mmolgDW−1h−1 (Section 2.2.2).
Arabitol (Section 2.3.3) rose together with glycerol (Fig. 4a–d)
but fell faster once glucose was depleted. In the two mixed
fermentations, its mean fell by about 80% from the last sample
with glucose to the first without, whereas mean glycerol fell by
less than 10%.
Glycerol peaked near glucose depletion, whereas dissolved
O2 recovered only after all sugars were depleted (Fig. 4a–c).
The two events coincided in Glc 15, lay within 5 h of each
other in Glc 10 + Fru 5 and lay at least 8 h apart in Glc 5
+ Fru 10. Only Glc 5 + Fru 10 therefore separates the two
events. In this condition, glycerol accumulated while dissolved
O2 stayed at about 150% air saturation or higher, and it was
removed during growth on fructose while dissolved O2 stayed
near 110%. Glucose was depleted at about 2.2 gL−1 biomass,
compared with about 6 gL−1 in Glc 15, which is consistent with
the higher dissolved O2 in this condition. Glucose depletion at
lower biomass is also consistent with the lower glycerol peak in
this condition.

and 0.16 h−1 with dissolved O2 at 170 and 181% air saturation.

3.3.2. Medium acidification follows from growth stoichiometry
The fall in medium pH during growth is consistent with
ammonium assimilation as the main source of acidification in
this medium. Medium pH fell from about 6.6 to about 6.0
(Fig. 5c–f, Section 2.2.3). A charge balance with the buffer and
measured acids alone did not reproduce this fall and predicted a
nearly constant pH, with an RMSE of 0.221 (Fig. 5a). Adding
the proton released on ammonium assimilation produced a fall,
but one that was too large, with an RMSE of 0.765. Correcting
activitiesfortheionicstrengthofthemediumreducedtheRMSE
to 0.056, and adding phosphate and sulfate uptake increased the
RMSE slightly to 0.068. No parameter was fitted to pH.
A mid-growth bias and a rebound in pH remain unexplained.
The full balance predicts pH about 0.13 units above the measure-
ment in mid-growth (Fig. 5b). It also does not reproduce the
rebound in pH after sugar depletion in Glc 15 (Fig. 5c). Because
the balance uses online biomass, it tests the chemistry and is not
a forward prediction.
4. Discussion
Our hypothesis held for both how much biomass forms and
when it forms, the two quantities that a model for mycopro-
tein process design must estimate. Rule-based curation alone
broughtpredictedfinalbiomassclosertomeasurementinoxygen-
enriched batch fermentation without fitted parameters, and se-
quential uptake and lag time brought the biomass time courses
into close agreement. Lag time was fitted to the same time
courses, so the timing result shows agreement rather than predic-
tion. The informative result is that a fitted lag time alone could
not align the mixed fermentations without sequential uptake.
The advantage over the Draft model lies in biomass, and both
models described sugars equally well. The main remaining
discrepancyisanoverpredictionoffinalbiomassby6–9%inthe
fermentations containing glucose.
These results apply to oxygen-enriched batch fermentation
on glucose and fructose at one sugar level. Sequential uptake
of glucose and fructose is consistent with carbon catabolite
repression in filamentous fungi [37]. A kinetic model of the
same fermentations also assumed that glucose is preferred [4].
ThatmodeldescribedsugarsmorecloselythantheCuratedmodel
and biomass less closely, with 15 fitted parameters compared
with four. The two approaches are complementary, because
the kinetic model describes sugar consumption better and the
Curated model estimates yield before any data are fitted.
Thedirectionalityandcarrierrules,andnotenergy-generating
cycles, set the yield here. Energy-generating cycles are common
in uncurated models and typically inflate maximal biomass
production by 25% [10]. In this study, the cycle tests did not
determinetheyield. Modelsthatfailedthemretainedtheyieldof
theCuratedmodelwhenthedirectionalityandcarrierruleswere
in place, and the Curated model without the carrier rule passed
everytestwithanATPyieldabovethetheoreticalmaximum. The
role of directionality fits the view of that study that such errors
can reflect wrong assumptions about reversibility. The carrier
direction matters because mitochondrial ATP could otherwise
reachthecytosolasGTP,withouttheprotoncostoftheADP/ATP
carrier. Directionality and transport errors can inflate ATP yield
in this way, and curated models ofEscherichia coli, yeast and
human metabolism are therefore checked against realistic ATP
yields [47–49]. With this cost in place the P/O ratio lies close
to measured values [34] and to the ratios set in iMA871 [29],
the source of the growth-associated maintenance. Maximum
ATPyieldshouldthereforebetestedalongsideenergy-generating
cycles when a model is transferred from a related species.
A lower P/O ratio and unmeasured products may each explain
part of the remaining discrepancy on glucose. Products remain-
ing at the end of the fermentation cannot explain it, because
glycerolandarabitolconcentrationsreturntoaboutzero. Alower
P/Oratiocouldcontribute,becauseeffectiveP/Oratiosbelowthe
mechanistic value have been reported for the filamentous fungus
Penicillium roquefortiin solid-substrate culture [50], and a ratio
of 2.0 brought the three fermentations containing glucose within
3%. The same ratio underpredicted Fru 15, so a single P/O
ratio cannot explain both sugars. Yield data alone also cannot
separate the P/O ratio from growth-associated maintenance [51].
Unmeasured products may also contribute, because medium
phosphate masks pyruvic and citric acids (Section 2.3.3). The
mid-growth bias in pH and the rebound after sugar depletion
are both consistent with an unmeasured acid that forms during
growth and is consumed once sugars are depleted. Polyols
other than these two are a further candidate, because deleting a
mannitol-1-phosphate dehydrogenase in anotherF. venenatum
strain decreased supernatant mannitol and increased biomass by
9% [52]. A growth-maximising model secretes a by-product
only when a constraint makes its secretion part of the optimal
solution, as the capped O2 uptake did for acetaldehyde and
ethanol (Section 3.3.1). Without oxygen or nitrogen limitation,
carbon secreted as a by-product is unavailable for growth, so
the optimal solution contains no by-product unless a further
constraint makes its secretion optimal. Glycerol formed at high
dissolvedO2 andnotonfructoseatsimilargrowth,whichargues
against oxygen limitation and suggests a regulatory effect that a
growth-maximising model does not contain. Because removal
afterglucosedepletionwasaboutasfastasformation,itsabsence
on fructose could also reflect immediate reuse.
These conclusions are limited by their scope, inputs and
measurements. They rest on one strain, four conditions, two
sugars and one sugar level, with three replicates each. Within
that scope, four inputs could bias the comparison. Maintenance
is borrowed, cell wall, lipid, DNA and the small-molecule pool
are not measured in this strain, lag time is fitted to the same
data, and initial sugars are nominal, with sugar concentrations
scaled to them. The measurements also have limits. CO2 was
notmeasured,sothecarbonbalancecannotbeclosed,andonline
biomassiscalibratedonlyatitsendpoints. Theyieldcomparison
neverthelessrestsonthegravimetricfinaldryweight,whichdoes

5. Conclusions
iJL1143, the Curated model ofF. venenatumA3/5, predicts
finalbiomassinoxygen-enrichedbatchfermentationwithamean
discrepancy of 6% without fitted parameters, compared with
20%fortheDraftmodel. Sequentialsugaruptakeandafittedlag
timereduceitsbiomasstime-courseRMSEbymorethantenfold.
In oxygen-enriched batch fermentation, it can therefore estimate
the biomass obtainable from a sugar feedstock and describe the
course of a batch fermentation. Groups building a model for a
related fungus should apply thermodynamic directionality rules,
check the proton cost of mitochondrial ATP export, and test
maximum ATP yield as well as energy-generating cycles before
anycomparisonwithdata. Theyshouldalsoaddsequentialsugar
uptake and lag time before judging time courses.

Supplementary Note 1. Lag time in batch fermentation
Fitted initial biomass sets the timing of growth and leaves final biomass nearly unchanged. Initial biomass was fitted to the time
to half final biomass of each condition, with sequential uptake in the mixed fermentations (Section 2.2.1, Supplementary Fig. S1).
Fitted values for the Curated model ranged from5.5×10 −5 to 1.1×10 −3 gL−1, and one shared value was2.7×10 −4 gL−1. The
mean discrepancy in final biomass was 6.2% at the assumed value and 6.1% when fitted.

Supplementary Note 2. Sensitivity of the yield
Yield depends mainly on maintenance and the P/O ratio, and little on the assumed sugar𝐾m. Settings are given in Section 2.2.2,
and the change for each input in Supplementary Fig. S2. The Curated model predicts 0.54 gg−1 on both sugars, close to the measured
yieldof0.53gg −1 onfructose. Reachingthemeasuredyieldonglucosewouldrequiregrowth-associatedmaintenancetobeincreased
1.4-fold or non-growth-associated maintenance 3.7-fold. Reducing the P/O ratio for NADH from 2.46 to 2.25 and 2.0 results in a
maximum of 27.5 and 24.7 mol ATP per mol glucose. The yields are then 0.53 and 0.51 gg−1. Across a𝐾m of 0.025–4.5 mM, final
biomass changed by less than 1%. With initial biomass held at its fitted value, the time to half final biomass moved by less than 0.3 h
at 0.025 mM and by up to 9 h at 4.5 mM.
Supplementary Note 3. Identity of the 16.1 min peak
The 16.1 min peak matches an arabitol standard in retention time. Arabitol, xylitol, sorbitol, mannitol and malic acid standards at
1 gL−1 were injected on the same instrument and column, with the same mobile phase, flow and column temperature, in a sequence
run before the fermentations. Retention times of that sequence were aligned to each fermentation run with glucose and glycerol as
reference peaks. An alignment of this kind, tested on arabitol between two standard sequences, erred by 0.04 min. In Glc 15 the peak
