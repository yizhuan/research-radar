## Top Developments

### 1. Google DeepMind rolls out Gemini 4 Neon to developers, businesses and paid subscribers (Google)
Google DeepMind released Gemini 4 Neon to developers, businesses and paid Gemini subscribers, bringing the model to the Gemini app and API. Google says Neon handles coding, research and analytical tasks and can process spreadsheets, PDFs, charts, audio and video together into structured documents. Access varies by product: Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers can select Neon in the Gemini app, businesses can use it through Gemini for business, and developers get it via the paid Gemini API tier in Google AI Studio and Android Studio. Google quotes $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, with cached input at $0.15 per million and a 1 million-token context window. Google claims first place on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index at 58.6, 65.2% on Terminal-Bench 4.1 and 46.2% on FrontierCode 2.0 at default high setting, and third overall on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at 61 at xhigh thinking. A Neon for cyber version is to reach trusted defenders through the Fairwind Program over the coming days.
Why it matters: A frontier-lab flagship release with published pricing, benchmark claims and multi-surface availability is the day's most consequential product event, and it lands as the fourth Gemini 4 model in roughly seven weeks.

### 2. OpenAI tests Dots agents with ChatGPT Plus users and rolls them out to Business Standard (OpenAI)
OpenAI began testing its Dots personal AI agents with a small number of ChatGPT Plus users and started rolling them out to eligible ChatGPT Business Standard members in the United States. Dots are proactive agents that can perform tasks and keep working when the user is not interacting with them, with access to OpenAI's plugin ecosystem and more than 4,000 apps, and can connect to and control a user's computer. Dots initially ran on GPT-6 Astra and now also support GPT-6.1 Terra. OpenAI is also testing a redesigned ChatGPT mobile app with a dedicated dot tab, starting with Pro users who have or can create a dot plus a small group of eligible Plus users. Dots are not available in the European Economic Area, the UK or Switzerland.
Why it matters: Moving always-on agents from the most expensive plans to the $20 Plus tier and to business seats is the clearest signal yet that OpenAI wants proactive agents to be a mass-market default rather than a premium add-on.

### 3. DeepSeek restricts free-user conversations and document reading, days after opening a full application API suite (DeepSeek)
Since 15 November, DeepSeek users have encountered notices that long conversations inactive for 72 hours can no longer be processed and that long documents are limited to the first 20 pages or 10,000 tokens, both attributed to a shortage of computing resources. The restrictions drew complaints under DeepSeek's official Xiaohongshu post, with some users asking for the limits to be removed and offering to pay for a subscription; unlike Doubao and Kimi, DeepSeek offers no paid consumer membership and charges through its API instead. The squeeze follows DeepSeek's 13 November announcement of application APIs covering online search, deeper-reasoning search, file input, model safety and underlying functions such as text and image search, webpage retrieval, text recognition and speech synthesis, billed per call beyond token charges. Analysts quoted by Caixin say DeepSeek has fewer compute resources than major cloud providers and is prioritising paying customers.
Why it matters: A leading Chinese lab visibly rationing consumer usage while monetising developer tooling captures the shift from chasing benchmark leadership toward ecosystem and revenue, and it is a concrete capacity constraint rather than a roadmap claim.

### 4. Viral claim ties a doubling of global cyber incidents to Zhipu's open-weight GLM-5.3 release (GLM)
A widely circulated post attributed to @unusual_whales and citing Bridgewater research says annualised global cyber incidents rose from roughly 4,000 to nearly 10,000 on a 60-day annualised basis, with the inflection coinciding with Zhipu AI's open-weight release of GLM-5.3. The same account says verified AI-driven incidents rose from near zero to more than 200 per year, including attacks that stole South Korean bank and credit-card data, and that Anthropic's red team found GLM-5.3 could construct working exploits. The report itself states that whether GLM-5.3 is the cause remains uncertain and that the increase could stem from a broader mid-2026 wave of agentic tooling, noting that correlation in timing does not establish causation.
Why it matters: If the underlying figures hold, this is the most concrete public case yet where an open-weight release is framed simultaneously as a competitive and a security-policy question, with a rival lab's red team as the independent check.

### 5. UK MP sues xAI (now SpaceXSI) over Grok-generated sexualised images (xAI)
Jess Asato, Labour MP for Lowestoft, is suing SpaceXSI — formerly known as xAI — for damages after sexualised images and videos, including some depicting her being sexually assaulted, were generated and shared online using Grok. Her claim alleges the company's design choices make it responsible for Grok's output and that it misused her private information and breached data protection rules. According to the MP, SpaceXSI filed a defence on 30 October admitting the chatbot is designed to expand on short user prompts by pulling data from X threads and the wider internet to create deepfakes. The report also describes a Grok "spicy mode" whose system prompts contained "no restrictions on adult sexual content or offensive content", later restricted to paying subscribers, and notes that creating or requesting a non-consensual deepfake of an adult is illegal in the UK.
Why it matters: A named legislator suing an AI developer over product design, with an admitted design description in the defence, puts liability for generative output squarely in front of a court rather than a regulator.

### 6. SemiAnalysis publicly retracts its August "Gemini is cooked" call and apologises to Google DeepMind (Google)
SemiAnalysis posted an apology form addressed to Google DeepMind chair Demis Hassabis on X, formally retracting its August 2026 pessimistic assessment of Google's model prospects and amending its headline from "Gemini is cooked" to the effect that Gemini has now delivered. The original report had argued Google had fallen behind, citing departures from DeepMind's reinforcement-learning team and poor internal compute allocation, and judged the probability of returning to the top tier as having fallen to zero. The retraction follows Google's Gemini 4 Argon, Carbon, Hydrogen and Neon releases, with Carbon briefly topping the Artificial Analysis composite leaderboard. DeepMind researcher Arthur Douillard said the team had printed the critical article and kept it on the desk as a daily reminder, and Hassabis reposted the apology, accepting it while joking that the checklist missed "underestimating the people at Google DeepMind".
Why it matters: A prominent independent analyst reversing a categorical judgment in public is a rare, checkable data point on how fast frontier standings can move within a single quarter.

### 7. Anthropic publishes Claude Science tooling documentation covering code execution, environments and GPUs (Anthropic)
Anthropic's Claude.ai documentation describes Claude Science, in which Claude writes and runs Python, R and shell commands (PowerShell on Windows), with Python and R running in a persistent kernel that keeps variables in memory across steps and ends after about 30 minutes idle, on a package-install restart, or at session end. First launch creates two read-only conda starter environments in ~/.claude-science; when work needs more packages, Claude reuses a named environment or proposes a new one behind a permission card, and environments are shared across all projects on the machine. Package installs default to micromamba from conda-forge, bioconda, defaults and pytorch channels, pip from PyPI, and CRAN and Bioconductor for R, with an option to point installs at an internal mirror. The sandbox has no root access or system package manager, code can read but not modify granted folders, and on Linux machines with NVIDIA GPUs a Host GPU switch in Settings > Compute makes GPUs available to Claude-run code while reducing the default sandboxing configuration.
Why it matters: Documented, permission-gated local code execution with GPU access is the substrate that makes agentic scientific work usable inside an enterprise security model, and it is the most concrete Anthropic artifact in the window.
Refs: https://claude.com/docs/claude-science/tools-and-environments

### 10. Moonshot publishes Kimi K3.1 Pro API documentation and an advisor-tool pattern (Kimi)
Moonshot's Kimi API platform documents kimi-k3.1-pro as its flagship model for long-horizon coding and end-to-end knowledge work, with native visual understanding, a 1-million-token context window, reasoning effort settings of low, high and max (default max), and a high-speed variant. The platform offers Chat Completions, Responses and Anthropic-compatible Messages interfaces, plus file, batch, search and fetch endpoints. A separate guide describes wrapping a flagship model as a client-side advisor tool so a lightweight executor such as kimi-k2.6 can consult kimi-k3.1-pro on difficult debugging or design decisions during long-horizon tasks, with advice to cap consultations per task and to strip the executor's reasoning content before the advisor request.
Why it matters: The advisor pattern is a concrete cost-control design for agentic coding, and the documentation is the clearest in-window artifact of how Chinese labs are competing on harness design rather than raw model scores.

## Takeaway

The competitive axis this cycle is not raw model scores but whether agents are cheap, controllable and auditable enough to ship by default. Watch for published usage limits and regional availability for the newly widened agent tiers, and for any independent replication of the open-weight security claims.
